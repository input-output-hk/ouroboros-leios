Summary​

This report measures the Leios pipeline on the public testnet and on the benchmarking cluster at three parameter sets, named by the reference list an endorser block may carry and the bytes of its closure. Every transaction measured is a value transfer without scripts. On the testnet the build is measured on one host, which validates every EB in time (section 9.2); its effect at the committee's scale is still to be measured.

Section 5 measures a cost to Praos on the build of section 4, a slower forge path and a higher fork rate: the CIP's reapplication constraint holds at 260 kB closures and fails from 555 kB up. The latest build restores it at 1 MB where it has run (section 9.3).

What is not yet measured on the latest build. No cluster run exists at the 308 kB / 2 MB set, and the testnet's committee has not run the build. Further experiments on both are scheduled, and this report makes no claim about the points they would measure.

Section 8 estimates what the latest build would do where it has not run. It reprices the votes measured on the build of section 4 at the latest build's validation time. The same repricing predicted 97 % for the cluster run before that run existed, and the run gave 99 %. On the testnet's 2 MB windows the repricing takes validation out of the causes of late votes at every host class and EB size (section 8.5).

What remains at 2 MB is the closure's arrival. On the build of section 4 it keeps 22 % of the votes late on the largest EBs of one window (Table 8.2). Changes aim at it, forwarding the closure as it arrives and scheduling what a relay sends among them. None is measured in this report (section 8.4).

What the measurements do not cover. No window or run in this report exercised mainnet's transaction mix, with scripts. The comparison with the targets of section 1 is therefore in bytes per second at the measured transactions, not at mainnet's mix. The latest build frees validation time that script computation would use. How much of it scripts take is a measurement still to make.

Mainnet baseline and targets​

Before any Leios measurement is read, two things have to be fixed: what Praos delivers on mainnet today, both on paper and under real congestion, and what the targets for Leios are once they are expressed in the same unit. The results in the later sections are read against the numbers established here.

Praos on paper and on the chain​

A Praos block is limited to 90,112 bytes, and the chain produces one every 20 seconds on average, with the active slot coefficient f at 0.05. The protocol therefore carries at most 4.5 kB/s. Chain density, the share of the expected blocks that extend the chain, is 97 to 99 % in every period below but one, so this ceiling is nearly the real rate. Every Leios parameter set is a multiple of it.

Three observations from this table are used throughout the report.

First, mainnet has been saturated for days only once, at the SundaeSwap launch of January 2022. Every congestion since Vasil, the upgrade of September 2022, has lasted hours, with full blocks alternating with near-empty ones as the mempool drains faster than it refills: the median block is full while the mean is 57 to 78 %. The best sustained rate since Vasil is 3.4 kB/s. This is why the report measures Leios over congested hours rather than days.

Second, transaction size is a range, not a constant: the mean runs from 461 B under the spam campaign to 2,396 B in the minting wave. A target stated as a count has no meaning without a transaction size, so the report states every target in bytes per second and converts it at three sizes.

Third, chain density falls under load, from 99 % in normal operation to 94 % in the one sustained full-block period. A certifying Leios block carries more work at adoption than a Praos block; section 5 measures what that does to density.

The targets​

The throughput targets come from the two documents that commissioned Leios, the treasury proposal and CIP-164, and are taken as stated.

The treasury proposal, "IO: Consensus Initiative", commits to 27 million transactions a month by 2030, from about 800,000 today, and asks for at least six times the current capacity. Elsewhere it states that Leios delivers ten times or more. Its unit is a count, about three transactions per second, the Praos ceiling at 1,500 B per transaction. The report reads the multipliers at the mainnet size mix, where six and ten times are 27 and 45 kB/s.

CIP-164 quotes throughput at 1,500 B per transaction. Its cost-estimate document gives 50 transactions per second as the sustainability line. Its simulations give 150 to 350 kB/s for the parameter set it describes as feasible, with 512 kB endorser blocks and 12 MB closures.

The proposal treats the CIP's parameters as an ideal goal: feasibility may be confirmed at a lower parameter set, with the higher targets reached through the gradual deployment plan. That is the standard this report applies: a parameter set confirmed feasible, with a credible path upward to the proposal's multipliers.

Bytes are one of several limits on a Leios block. Plutus steps, Plutus memory and reference-script bytes are capped alongside them, and section 2 states which of these the measurements exercise. The congested periods of Table 1.1 are characterised in bytes only. Whether the proposed sets scale the script budgets from the Praos limits is not settled here. The validation margin that script computation would draw on is discussed in sections 8.5 and 9.

Where the measured parameter sets sit on paper​

The report measures three parameter sets. Each is named by the two caps that define it, in the symbols of CIP-164. The first, S_EB, caps the bytes of the reference list an endorser block may carry, and so its transaction count, at 36 or 37 bytes per reference depending on the transaction's size. The second, S_EB-tx, caps the bytes of its closure, the transactions it brings into the chain.

What is assessed and what to expect​

This section describes the Leios pipeline as deployed on the measured networks: its stages, the limits that bound it, and how a parameter set's ceiling relates to what the chain carries. It holds no measurements. It fixes the vocabulary with which section 4 is read.

The pipeline and its clock​

Leios adds to Praos a second kind of block, the endorser block. The ranking block (RB) remains the Praos block and the only one that extends the chain. The endorser block (EB) is a list of transaction references announced inside an RB. Its transactions, called its closure, enter the ledger when a later RB carries a certificate from a committee of stake pools that validated them. Between announcement and certificate the pipeline has six stages, each with a clock of its own.

The first stage is the forger's snapshot. The forger, the pool elected for the slot, snapshots its mempool, revalidating its transactions against the ledger state at the slot, builds the RB and announces with it an EB of the snapshot's transactions that fit the caps. The snapshot has a time budget, so the EB is bounded by the mempool's content and by that budget, not only by the caps. As deployed, an RB that carries a certificate carries no transactions of its own.

The second and third stages are diffusion. Every node fetches the EB body, the reference list, and then assembles the closure from its own mempool and from its peers.

The fourth stage is validation and voting. The committee, the pools with the most stake with each vote weighted by stake, validates the closure against the ledger state and votes. The vote is scheduled three seconds after the EB's slot and must be cast by seven. A voter that receives the closure after the deadline does not vote.

Validation applies every transaction of the closure to the ledger state: in full for the ones the node has not seen, and as a cheaper reapplication for the ones it validated at mempool admission or in an earlier EB. Its cost therefore depends on how much of the EB the voter had already validated.

The fifth stage is the tally. The EB is certifiable once the votes reach the quorum, three quarters of the committee's weight. The certificate can only go in an RB at least fourteen slots after the announcing one, the certification gap. On paper the next RB comes that late with probability 0.513, so about half of the announced EBs are eligible for certification. The rest are superseded by the next announcement.

The sixth stage is adoption. A node that receives a certifying RB must hold the closure and apply its transactions before adopting the block. That is more work than a plain Praos block takes, and if it delays propagation the next slot leader may fork the chain. Section 5 is about this stage.

Each stage has a deadline or a budget. The tables of section 4 have one row per stage for that reason.

Two timings follow from the three periods. The vote window opens 3 L_hdr after the EB's slot and closes at 3 L_hdr + L_vote. The certification gap, the earliest the certificate can be included, is 3 L_hdr + L_vote + L_diff after the announcing RB, 14 slots at the deployed values. Underneath them Praos keeps its own parameters: the block limit, maxBlockBodySize at 90,112 B, and the active slot coefficient f, which set the plain block's share and the RB rate.

The measurements exercise the two caps, the timings at their deployed values, the certification gap at 14 slots and the committee with each network's real stake. The Praos block limit and f stay at mainnet's values. The script and reference-script budgets are left untouched: every transaction measured carries no script.

Three conditions outside the parameter set bound the pipeline as much as the parameters do: the hardware of the nodes, which decides how fast a closure is validated; the mempool coverage, how much of an EB a node already holds when it is announced, which decides how much of it is applied in full; and the topology, which decides how fast bodies, closures and votes travel. This report measures on the populations of section 3 and states its results against them. It defines no reference machine.

The script budgets are not independent of the byte caps. A voter must have received and validated the closure by the deadline, so the closure's arrival and its validation share the same seconds. Whatever validation work a closure carries, script computation or more transactions, must be paid for by a closure that arrives earlier, and at a given bandwidth an earlier closure is a smaller one. At fixed hardware, bandwidth and implementation, a larger script budget therefore means a smaller closure. This report treats the script budgets as values to be set conservatively alongside the caps it measures, not as dimensions it has confirmed.

The ceiling and the ladder​

The rate a parameter set can carry on paper follows from the pipeline above. Each RB either certifies the EB announced by the previous one, with probability p, and brings its closure of up to S_EB-tx bytes, or does not and carries a plain block of up to R bytes, the Praos block limit. RBs come at the rate f per slot. The parameter ceiling is therefore

f × (p × S_EB-tx + (1 − p) × R)

bytes per second, with p = 0.513 under the fourteen-slot gap and R = 90,112 B, against which the plain block's fullness is also read in the rungs below. It assumes every EB full and every eligible EB certified. The chain carries less than this for reasons that can be named one at a time, and the report presents them as a ladder of rungs, each a correction applied on top of the previous one:

Load and mempool coverage​

Load, for this report, is what reaches a forger's mempool when its slot comes, not what users submitted to the network. The distinction matters twice.

For the forger, the EB is bounded by the mempool at the slot. A generator that submits in bursts, or to a few nodes, produces EBs under the caps whatever the parameters. The load-adjusted rung separates that shortfall from the protocol's.

For the voters, validation cost depends on the share of the EB's transactions they already hold. A transaction that reached the voter's mempool before the announcement is reapplied cheaply. One that did not is applied in full. Leios therefore wants the same transaction in many mempools, where Praos needs it only in the next leader's.

How transactions are submitted on mainnet is not known from the testnet. The report makes no claim that the generators used are an optimistic or a pessimistic case for it. It reports the mempool coverage of each population beside its results, so that the reader can place mainnet against them.

The same distinction is why the report measures in load windows. The caps bound the long-run rate, but the mempools of a short burst's forgers decide how full that burst's EBs are. A burst of full EBs over a few minutes is not evidence of headroom, and a daily average mixes loaded hours with idle ones. Every measurement in section 4 is taken over the hours in which the generators kept the mempools loaded, with the windows stated.

What to expect at each stage​

The structure above gives an expectation for each stage before any measurement. Section 4 states them per parameter set, with the numbers the ceiling supplies.

Body and closure diffusion should scale with the bytes to move. They are bound by bandwidth on hosts that receive from well-connected peers, and by topology on hosts several hops from the forgers. A closure that arrives after the vote deadline is lost to the voting stage whatever else happens.

Validation should cost a roughly constant time per transaction the voter has not validated before. It therefore grows with the EB's transaction count and with the share the voter applies in full, and it has to fit in the four seconds between the vote mark and the deadline once the closure is in hand.

The tally should reach the quorum once three quarters of the committee's weight has voted. It arrives close to the deadline when the slow voters hold much of the weight. A quorum of 0.75 leaves a margin of 0.25, and every seat that is silent for any reason spends it.

Certification therefore depends on the network's stake distribution as much as on the parameters. A network where most of the weight sits on fast, well-connected hosts certifies EBs that a network with the same parameters and a wider spread of hosts does not.

Adoption of a certifying RB should cost the closure's reapplication on top of a plain block's adoption, growing with the closure. The consequence to look for is not a missed slot but a late block: the next leader building on the previous block because the certifying one had not reached it.

Where a stage falls short of its expectation in section 4, the report asks which of the three outside conditions, hardware, mempool coverage or topology, or which parameter, explains the shortfall, and what change would recover it. That is the content of sections 6 and 8.

Populations and instruments​

The measurements come from three sources: the public Leios testnet, the benchmarking cluster, and a ledger benchmark run by stake pool operators on their own machines. Each observes a different population under different conditions and answers a different part of the question. This section describes the three, the instruments that read them, and what each can and cannot say, so that the results of section 4 are read with their population in mind.

The benchmarking cluster​

The cluster is a network of 51 nodes run by the benchmarking team on one instance type, c5d.2xlarge (8 vCPUs, 15.2 GiB), each with six upstream peers in a fixed topology, an in-memory ledger, two capabilities, and tracing at debug level. The two 100 kB / 1 MB runs ran the same w42 revision as the testnet's first two windows. The 40 kB / 260 kB run ran an untagged commit of the same week, three days before the tag, which accepted a certificate from 15 slots after the announcing RB rather than 14. Every node is a block producer with the same stake, so the committee is 51 voters of equal weight and the quorum is 39 votes.

A generator feeds every node with value transactions of a fixed shape, different transactions to each node, saturating the caps throughout a run. The transactions spend outputs created at genesis, which the runs reuse almost never.

The ledger benchmark​

The third source is a benchmark of block validation that 26 stake pool operators ran on their own machines between 7 and 18 October 2026, with the consensus team's database analyser over one synthetic Praos chain. It times, per transaction, the application of a block to the ledger in full and its reapplication, with the UTxO set in memory and on disk. What it can say is the one thing neither network can: how far the operators' machines are apart. In-memory application spans a factor of six across the 26 machines, and storage adds a spread of its own.

The report uses it twice. Section 6 places the validation costs measured on the networks against it as the hardware axis. Section 7 reads the DeltaQ model's constants, which are costs of this kind, against the networks' measured costs through it.

Leios on Praos​

Leios leaves the Praos block in place. It adds work to the RB that carries a certificate: that RB makes a closure of transactions part of the ledger, so no node can validate it, or build on it, before it has applied the closure to its ledger state.

Section 2.1 put this stage last in the pipeline because it is the one stage on Praos's own path. This section measures what it cost on the runs of section 3, on the build of section 4.

Two comparisons are made. Within each run, the RB that certifies and announces an EB is set against the full plain RB that announces one, of the same nodes in the same hour. Both pay the mempool snapshot, so the difference between them is the certificate's, isolated from the hour's load. Across runs, the benchmarking team's Praos control, a release node on the same cluster with the same transaction shape, says how much later the Leios node forges and adopts its RBs than the release node does its blocks, averaged over a run with certifying and plain RBs together.

Section 5.1 states what to expect, 5.2 picks the RBs compared, 5.3 to 5.5 measure adoption, forks and chain density, 5.6 reads the control, and 5.7 says what the cost is made of. The tables are regenerated by scripts/praos_table.py.

What is expected​

A Praos block that arrives late costs no slot: the leader of the next slot forges regardless, on whatever chain it holds. If that leader has not adopted the previous block within the one second between the two slots, it builds on the block before. The two RBs then compete at the same height and the tiebreak orphans one of them. A slow block therefore costs a fork, not a missed slot. Two measures follow: how long the nodes take to adopt each kind of RB, and how often consecutive-slot leader pairs fork. Chain density, the RBs on the chain per slot, falls by the orphans and by nothing else as long as every leader forges.

Under load a full plain RB already takes most of that second to adopt. A certifying RB adds two steps to that path. The forger applies the certified closure to its ledger state before it can forge, and each adopter applies it again inside chain selection. Each step costs the closure's transaction count times what the ledger spends per transaction this way, and a plain RB pays neither.

CIP-164 calls this step the closure's reapplication, Δ_reapply, and defines it as the application with minimal checks and UTxO updates: the certificate licenses it, and it is not the voter's reapply of section 2.1, which keeps the ledger's checks. The CIP's security argument requires that step to cost less than a plain block's transaction validation, Δ_reapply < Δ_applyTxs, so that a certifying RB is processed within the same Δ_RB bound as a Praos block. That inequality, the CIP's reapplication constraint, is what this section tests.

The certifying RB has one advantage. Its body is about 1 kB against 88 kB, and applying a closure without validation costs less per transaction than validating a full block's transactions from scratch. At small closures it can therefore be adopted earlier than a full plain RB. The expectation is a crossover: the certifying RB is adopted earlier below some transaction count and later above it, by an amount that grows with the closure.

Above the crossover, consecutive-slot pairs should fork more often when the first RB certifies. No leader slot should be missed, and the chain's density should fall by the forks alone.

What this says and what would change it​

Leios costs Praos nothing in slots and something in forks. The something is the time to apply the certified closure at adoption: its transaction count times the ledger's cost per transaction applied this way, tens of microseconds on these value transactions.

At a thousand transactions the certifying RB is the faster block, and the CIP's reapplication constraint holds. At a few thousand it is the slower one, by 0.07 to 0.41 s at the median. The consecutive-slot pairs it leads then fork accordingly, nearly all of them on the cluster at 1 MB. A transaction mix with a higher cost per applied transaction moves the crossover down in count by the same factor.

Underneath both kinds the Leios build's forge path is 0.35 to 0.59 s behind the release node's on every block. That is the larger part of the gap at 260 kB, and plain and certifying RBs share it equally.

Both costs are the implementation's. The certificate's is two applications of a closure that every voter validated at the vote window, 14 slots or more before the certifying RB. An implementation that kept the ledger state from vote time for adoption would remove most of it. This report has no measurement of such a change. The forge path's delay is removed by the build of section 9.3 where that build has run.

Two paths, two costs​

This subsection sets the cost a voter pays per transaction at the vote, measured on the testnet in section 6.2 and on the cluster in section 7.2, against the ledger benchmark, path by path, to read how much of that cost is the ledger's own and how much is the path around it. The voting path is where the voter validates the closure before it votes. The adoption path is where the node reapplies the certified closure to the chain, the step section 5 timed.

Both paths end in the ledger's two calls per transaction, apply for a transaction the node has not validated before and reapply for one it has. The benchmark of section 7.1 times those two calls and nothing else. Around the calls each path does work of its own: it resolves the transaction's inputs, builds the state the call runs against and keeps the books of the closure as it goes. The voting path does this transaction by transaction in a loop. The measurements of sections 6.2 and 7.2 time the path as the node runs it, that work included.

The benchmark and the measurements therefore time different things, and the difference is the point. The benchmark gives the cost of the ledger call alone, the lowest a path could reach. The measurement gives what the path costs as observed. Their ratio on the same hardware is the path's own overhead. The survey's three Xeon Gold 6240 machines are of the c5d generation (section 7.1), so PIR7, PIR9 and the cluster's nodes can be read against that row of Table 7.1.

The adoption path adds little. The like-for-like reading is the cluster's two-input run, which adopts its certifying RBs at 102 to 114 µs per closure transaction (the adoption-path output of the run), against the benchmark's reapply of 47 to 49 µs on the same generation. The adoption path therefore costs about twice the ledger call inside it.

The voting path adds much more. The benchmark's transaction has two inputs and spends outputs of earlier blocks, so the one reading of the same shape is the cluster's two-input run (Table 7.2). There the loop applies at nineteen times the ledger's apply and reapplies at three times the ledger's reapply.

Everything between one and nineteen is the loop's own work around the ledger call. The loop's cost is what a voter pays per transaction today. It cannot fall below the ledger call inside it, so the ledger's cost is the least the loop could ever cost. The change of section 9 brings the same run's apply cost to about twice the ledger call, the adoption path's factor. Section 8 analyses what reducing the voting path's overhead would do for certification.

The CIP's cost model​

The CIP's cost model, the compute cost estimation per node, has constants fitted on mainnet and charges a node for two things. Apply is one full validation per transaction, paid when the transaction first reaches the node: 0.6201 ms whatever the size, the mean CPU time of a full apply over mainnet's blocks, so the average script load is in it. Reapply is one application of an EB's validated transactions to the ledger state, paid once per EB: 0.3539 ms per EB plus 0.00002151 ms per byte. Scripts are charged separately, 0.9487 ms per billion Plutus steps, when a budget is modelled explicitly. For a transaction of the testnet's size, 318 B, in a closure of a few thousand, the two terms come to 0.62 ms and about 7 µs.

Each term has its counterpart in the node. The full validation is the voter's apply of a transaction it has not seen, measured at the vote in sections 6.2 and 7.2 with the loop's own work inside it (section 7.3). The application of validated transactions is the adoption path's reapplication of the certified closure (sections 5 and 7.3). The voter's reapply at the vote of the transactions it had already seen keeps the ledger's checks and is a different operation (section 2.1); the model has no term for it, nor for the loop around the ledger calls. Scripts have no measurement in this report.

The measured apply brackets the model's 0.62 ms, with no script run at all. On every host class the chained cost sits under it, 0.16 to 0.37 ms. The old-input cost sits above it, 0.65 ms and up on the testnet's older hosts and 0.91 ms and up on the cluster. The change of section 9 removes most of the loop's own work and brings the voter's apply cost in line with the model's figure.

The adoption path costs more than the model's reapply term: 34 to 41 µs per one-input 318 B transaction on the IOG and PIR hosts and 102 to 114 µs per two-input transaction on the cluster, five to fourteen times the term. The ledger's own reapply call inside that pass is above the term as well, 23 to 120 µs for the benchmark's 368 B transaction across the survey's machines (Table 7.1) against the term's 8 µs. The operation the model does not represent, the voter's reapply at the vote, costs 79 to 163 µs per transaction (Tables 6.2a and 6.2b).

The script cost is unmeasured by every source here. The CIP's per-step figure is the only number for it.

The model's apply figure is therefore of the order the measurements find for transactions without scripts, and the report cannot say more, since no source here measures a script. Its reapply term is a fifth to a fourteenth of the adoption path's measured cost. The voter's reapply at the vote and the loop around the ledger calls are not represented. A rerun of the model with this report's constants, the apply and reapply costs of Tables 6.2a, 6.2b and 7.2 by host class and terms for the voting path, is an experiment this report has not made.

The DeltaQ model's inputs​

The DeltaQ model of certification, the improved ΔQ model for Linear Leios EB diffusion predicts from the voter's side whether an EB of a given closure size certifies within the vote window. Its voter has three inputs. Two are the costs of the voter's pass over the closure, 0.507 ms to apply a transaction it has not seen and 0.070 ms to reapply one it has, transaction-weighted means over a mainnet block set with script costs at their mainnet frequency. The third is the share of the closure the voter has to fetch and apply because it is not in its mempool: a sixth by default, about 0.06 on the model's own mainnet estimate. With those inputs the model finds the voter's CPU time under 2 s at 12 MB, the fetch of the missing share the dominant step, and the quorum met at every closure size up to 12 MB.

All three inputs have their counterpart in the measurements. The two costs are the voter's apply and reapply at the vote of sections 6.2 and 7.2, the loop's own work included (section 7.3). The missing share is the applied share at the closure, read at the median node in the per-node validation output of each testnet window and at the unfavourable-quartile node in the stage tables of section 4.

Against the testnet's hosts the two costs are placed well enough. The reapply figure is within about twice every host class, 79 to 163 µs against 70. The apply figure falls between the two measured cases, above the chained cost and under the old-input cost on every host class with a reading.

On the testnet the missing share is the median voter's, not the failing voter's. The median reporting node applies 16 to 22 % of an EB (the per-node validation output of each window), the model's sixth. The operator nodes past the deadline are the ones applying a quarter to half of an EB (Tables 4.6 and 6.1a to 6.1c).

Against the cluster every input is on the optimistic side. The model's apply, 0.507 ms, is under the cluster's 0.91 ms on the 260 kB run and 1.85 ms on the 1 MB run of the same one-input transaction (Table 7.2). Its reapply, 70 µs, is half the cluster's. Its missing share, a sixth of the closure, is under the 37 to 45 % the cluster's voters apply at the closure (Table 4.4).

Its verdict fares the same way. The model finds the quorum met at every closure size up to 12 MB. The cluster certified 69 % of its full EBs with 555 kB closures and 54 % with 1 MB closures (Table 4.3, the voting-adjusted rung).

The model's verdict therefore holds for a voter that holds most of the closure and pays the chained cost. The voters that decide certification on these populations pay the old-input cost on a quarter to half of the closure, a case the model's inputs do not describe.

This report's inputs to that model are the apply and reapply costs of Tables 6.2a, 6.2b and 7.2 by host class and input origin, the applied shares of Tables 4.4 and 4.6, and the closure's arrival of Tables 6.4 and 6.5 in place of the one-hop fetch. A run of the model on them is a check of the model against the populations measured: its certification verdict per closure size set against the measured rates of Tables 4.3 and 4.5. A second run at the chained apply cost, the voter's cost with the loop's own work removed (section 9), would estimate what that change buys. Neither run is made in this report.

What binds certification, and what would move it​

Sections 4 to 7 measured where the testnet and the cluster stand: at 260 kB closures nearly every EB certifies, at 1 and 2 MB about half do. Two things decide whether a voter makes the deadline. The first is the time it spends validating the closure before it votes. That is the loss on the cluster at 1 MB and in the testnet's 1 kB window, measured per transaction and per host. The second is when the closure reaches it. That is where the testnet's 318 B window loses its weight (section 4.4), and what remains once the validation cost is removed.

This section turns those measurements into a statement about the feasibility of the build measured in section 4: what binds certification at the measured sizes, which of the changes available to the implementation and the operators would move it, and by how much, in the report's own units.

Two budgets in one window​

A voter has seven seconds from the EB's slot to vote (section 2). Validation starts at the 3 s mark or when the closure arrives, whichever is later. The vote must be cast by 7 s, so the closure is validated in the last four seconds at most. The window therefore holds two budgets. The first is the closure's arrival: every second it takes past the 3 s mark is a second less to validate in. The second is the validation itself, the cost per transaction of section 6.2 times the share of the closure the voter has to apply (section 6.3), plus the reapply of the rest.

The levers the report can price​

Two levers act on the validation budget: faster hardware at the voters, and the removal of the voting loop's own cost. Both have a measured price per transaction. The hardware lever's is the cost per transaction by host class of Tables 6.2a and 6.2b. The loop lever's is the gap between the chained and the old-input apply cost, which section 9.1 traces to one step of the loop.

This section prices each lever on the testnet's 2 MB windows and the cluster's 1 MB runs, vote by vote. Each vote has a record of its validation: how many transactions the voter applied, how many it reapplied, and when its closure arrived. The repricing keeps the EB's composition and the closure's arrival and replaces only the cost per transaction, as if the voter had run on faster hardware or on a validation loop without its own cost, at the costs section 6 measured per host class and per closure kind. Each voter's validation time is then its applied count times the new apply cost plus its reapplied count times the new reapply cost, and the vote is in time if that validation ends by 7 s. The result reads what each lever would have done to the same votes on the same EBs.

On the cluster every node is observed, so the quorum says which EBs certify and the throughput follows.

On the testnet 0.37 to 0.39 of the committee's weight sends no logs and cannot be repriced, so the reading stops at the votes: how many of the reporting voters' votes are in time, and why the rest are late. No certification rate is projected from them. The unobserved voters run the same software on a similar spread of hardware, so either lever moves them in the same direction as the reporting ones. The per-vote reading says whether a lever removes validation as a cause of late votes or not.

The hardware lever is measured by repricing every vote at one host class's costs, from the oldest class the report measured, c5d, to the current generation. The apply cost taken is the class's old-input cost, the most expensive one measured, which section 6.2 says to plan on. Each class's row is therefore its pessimistic case. The hardware factor of a class is the ratio of its reapply cost to the c5d hosts' in Table 6.2b, 0.85 for c6id and 0.6 for the current generation.

The loop lever is measured by repricing the same votes at the class's chained apply cost instead, reapply unchanged. Section 9.1 traces the gap between the two apply costs to the loop's pass over the closure's input table, so the chained cost is the apply cost without that pass. Section 9 measures the change where it has run.

What the measurements rule out​

Starting validation when the closure arrives instead of at the 3 s mark recovers little: on the cluster it moves certification from 69 to 72 % on the one-input run and from 52 to 57 % on the two-input one (Table 8.4, last row). The closure arrives close to the 3 s mark at most voters, so the time gained is a fraction of a second against validation times of three to five.

Memory and sustained CPU bind nothing at the measured sizes. The testnet hosts' resident set is 1.7 to 2.2 GB at the median on hosts of 8 to 33 GB, with a ledger far smaller than mainnet's. The cluster's nodes hold a ledger of a million delegations at 13.8 GB on hosts of 15.2 GiB. The sustained CPU is 0.1 to 0.3 of a core at the median node of every population (Table 6.6). What binds is one core for the seconds of a validation, the cost 8.2 reprices.

One measured effect contributes to the voters' cost and is not a lever of the implementation: a voter that holds most of the closure before the EB arrives reapplies instead of applying (section 6.3). It depends on how the load is built and routed, not on the node, and the report plans without it. Where it occurs it is a benefit on top of the levers of 8.2, not part of the feasibility argument.

One loss sits outside every lever. The EBs at the S_EB cap whose forgers served the body to no reporting node fail before diffusion, and no voter speed touches them (section 4.3.1).

What the measurements leave as levers are therefore the two of 8.2 on the validation budget and the arrival levers of 8.4.

The levers the report cannot price​

The arrival budget has levers of its own, each with a measured target and no measured gain, so none has a row above.

Forwarding the closure as it arrives, before it is complete, and scheduling what a relay sends are aimed at the nodes with a single upstream relay. Their closure reaches the median such node at 9.30 s at the 90th percentile at 2 MB, against 3.08 to 4.33 s for nodes with two or more peers (Table 6.5). The gap is measured and the changes are not, so whether they recover it is a run to make.

Validating on more than one core is the largest unpriced lever on the validation side. Today the validation of a closure runs on one core: the cluster's nodes at two capabilities pay the same per transaction as the testnet's hosts at eight (section 7.2). The sustained CPU of Table 6.6, a minute's average over all cores, cannot show how busy a core is for the seconds of a validation.

Whether the loop can be parallelised is a question about the ledger's apply, which runs transactions in sequence. Which of its parts are independent, witness and signature checks among them, is not measured here, and the report makes no claim.

Section 8.4 therefore leaves two questions open, whether forwarding and scheduling recover the single-relay nodes' gap and whether the apply path parallelises, and neither has a measurement in this report.

What the measurements support​

As far as the transactions measured here represent, the measurements say three things about the feasibility of the build measured in section 4, at the sizes run.

At 260 kB closures the build certifies 97 % of its EBs on the testnet as it is, with every voter class and every closure kind (section 4, and the repricing of that window). Neither budget binds.

At 1 and 2 MB the validation budget binds: on the cluster at 1 MB, in the testnet's 1 kB window, and for the lighter voters of its 318 B window. The voting loop's own cost is the larger part of it. Removing that cost, to the chained level the report has measured, takes the cluster from 69 to 99 % certification at 1 MB on hosts three generations old. The quorum voter is then done by the middle of the window at the median. On the testnet's 2 MB windows it removes validation as a cause of late votes, from 10 and 8 % of the reporting voters' votes to under 2 % whatever the hardware. The build of section 9 removes that cost where it has run.

What that buys a network in certification depends on where its weight sits (section 2.5). On the 1 kB window the weight is lost at validation, so the change addresses the loss itself, by an amount the unobserved weight leaves unprojected. On the 318 B window the voters it flips hold 0.03 of the committee's weight, the gap between the weight holding the closure and the weight voting in Table 4.7.

What remains at 2 MB is the closure's arrival. The 318 B window loses its weight there (Table 4.7). With the loop's cost removed the votes still late are late arrivals, 15 of the 17 %. The levers for the arrival are the unpriced ones of 8.4.

In section 1's units, the cluster's 1 MB runs with the loop's cost removed carry 16 and 26 kB/s, against a six-times target of 27 kB/s and a ten-times target of 45 (Table 1.2). The testnet's 2 MB windows have no repriced figure, since their reading is per vote. They stand at their measured 12 and 22 kB/s (Table 4.5).

The comparison is in bytes per second at the measured transactions, not at mainnet's mix. The transactions measured are value transfers of one or two inputs without scripts. A like-for-like reading against mainnet needs a run of mainnet-like transactions, which the report has not made.

The measurements therefore find the build measured feasible at 260 kB as it is. At 1 MB feasibility depends on the loop's cost being removed, measured in section 9. At 2 MB the same change makes the validation time manageable. The arrival levers appear needed on the 2 MB set, unmeasured in this report.

A change to the voting loop, and where it has run​

The drafting of this report led to a review of the voting loop's code. The source of the old-input cost section 7.2 measures was identified, patched and measured. The patch reached one testnet host and one cluster run when the previous sections were mostly complete, so this section gives partial data on what section 8 claims the loop's cost removed would buy: a preview of the patch's effect, not its assessment. The change's effect on the testnet as a whole, and the cost a deployment can tolerate once it is in, are left to further work.

The change​

The voting loop carried the closure's input table inside the ledger state and made a pass over the whole table after every applied transaction. On an old-input closure the table holds one entry per transaction, on a chained one the heads of the chains, and the reapply path never makes the pass. That is why the old-input cost grows with the EB in section 7.2 and the other two costs do not. The patch keeps the table outside the ledger state and hands each transaction its own inputs, which removes the pass. It is local to the loop. It is merged in the consensus repository's Leios branch as commit 7b91f3619.

The change has run on one testnet host and one cluster run. What it does at the committee's scale is still section 8's repricing: the cost per transaction is confirmed on one host, the votes of the committee are not.

The host that ran it is of the oldest class measured, so the current generation's cost with the change is the repricing's figure and not a reading.

Those measurements are further work.

Appendix: how each quantity was measured​

This appendix states, for every quantity the report's tables and figures rest on, which records it was read from, what marks its start and its end, which records were kept or dropped, and how the result was aggregated, so that a reviewer can judge the approach and not only the numbers. Its first part describes the sources and the records they hold, the second the conventions every entry shares, the third the quantities themselves, and the fourth maps each quantity to the script or query that implements it.

Sources and their records​

The testnet chain. One row per RB on the final chain, from the testnet explorer's block API: block number, slot, hash, time, transaction count, size in bytes, whether the RB carries a certificate, the hash and reference bytes of the EB it announces, the closure bytes of the EB it certifies, and the vote share the explorer saw. For a certifying RB the transaction count is the certified closure's. The cache is refetched from the tip at each sync and a rolled-back tail is replaced. Everything the report computes "from the chain alone" reads this cache: the ladders, the sizes of EBs and closures, the certification rate, the RB kinds and the pairs on the chain.

The committee. One seat per pool per epoch, ranked by active stake descending with ties broken by the pool id, the seat's weight being the pool's share of the total stake, together with the epoch's Leios parameters (S_EB, S_EB-tx, N_c, the quorum threshold, the period lengths). The stake comes from the explorer's stake distribution for the epochs up to 2026-10-27 and from a db-sync instance from 2026-10-28, with the newest epoch common to both recomputed and matched seat for seat before any later epoch was written.

The operator telemetry. An agent on each participating operator node ships the node's event log and its metric scrapes to an archive. The report reads one record per event: its time, its namespace, its payload, and the pool and agent that sent it. The payload fields used are named entry by entry in the quantities. The metric scrapes give the node's build version and revision, resident memory, CPU time and mempool content.

The IOG monitoring. The same node events for the 13 IOG and PIR producers, in the IOG monitoring's log store, pulled per host and per event family: the voting family (vote scheduled, EB validated, closure acquired, body hits, not voted), the diffusion family (announcement accepted, body acquired), the votes family (votes cast and votes received with the running tally), the forge-loop events (leader, forged, adopted, not adopted), the chain-selection and block-fetch events, and, for the 2 MB period, the call traces of the forge loop and of chain selection with each call's name, argument and duration. For each testnet window the pulls are merged with the archive into one period database in the archive's record layout, the one IOG host that also sends to the archive being taken from the monitoring copy only, so that every instrument reads both populations with one definition. Two further pulls from the monitoring serve section 6 and are not merged into the period databases: the per-peer announcement events (one event per peer that announced an EB to the host, with the peer's connection id), and the per-minute metric series of each host (the node's resident memory, GC live data and CPU tick counters, and the host's CPU count, total memory and CPU busy fraction). The hosts' instance types, vCPU counts and CPU generations (Table 3.2) are held in a small table the instruments read.

The cluster runs. The benchmarking team's archives hold every node's traces in per-namespace tables with the node and the nanosecond time of each event. The report rebuilds from them the two inputs the testnet instruments take: the event records of the namespaces the instruments read, with the node's name as pool and agent, and a chain cache in the testnet's layout. The chain is reconstructed by taking the highest block that became any node's tip and walking parents back to the first block; RB sizes come from the forger's adoption event, EB sizes and transaction counts from the EB forged event, the certificate from the certified-and-announced event, and the closure bytes as the transaction count times the run's transaction size (the mode of the mempool's size increments). A plain RB's own transaction count is not traced on the cluster and is estimated from its size.

Mainnet. Block headers of whole epochs from a public block API (height, time, size, transaction count, forging pool) with each epoch's protocol parameters, read for the congestion windows of section 1.

The benchmarking team's comparison. The figures of Table 5.5 are taken as printed from the team's report of 2026-10-27, whose data dictionary defines each of them; the report does not recompute them.

The ledger benchmark survey. The operators' submissions to the ledger-operations survey of October 2026 (db-analyser at one pin over one chain fragment, 26 operators' machines and the benchmarking team's reference host), copied from the survey's results page with the machine names removed: per machine the processor model, logical CPUs and physical cores, virtualisation, the disk's write-cache mode, and the read latency, apply and reapply costs per transaction with the UTxO set in memory and on disk. Held as a small table the printer reads.

db-sync. The testnet's db-sync instance, reached through the monitoring's data source, gives the certified closures what the chain cache cannot: for every certifying RB of a window, the transactions it carries with their sizes, and for every input the block in which the spent output was created. The report keeps one row per certifying RB (transaction count and bytes, the size percentiles and extremes, the share of inputs spending an output of the same block and the median age in blocks of the outputs spent); the 1 kB window's composition table of section 4.3.1 is an earlier cut of the same query.

Conventions​

  • Slot clock. The testnet's slot 0 is 2026-10-18 00:00 UTC and a slot is one second; a cluster run's slot 0 is the system start its nodes logged. "Age after the slot" is the event's time minus the start of the slot of the RB or EB concerned. The chain's block times agree with their slot numbers to the second in every window used (the spread between the two is zero). Node events carry the node's own clock; the diffusion ages are computed by the node itself against the slot start, and the adoption delays of section 5 subtract the slot start from the node's timestamp, so a node with a wrong clock would show as an outlier in both, and none did.
  • Loaded windows. Each testnet window is the set of hours in which the load generator ran (Table 3.1), kept as a list of start and end times; an EB or RB belongs to a window by the time of its slot. Cluster runs are read whole. A window's transaction size (Table 3.1) is the bytes of its certified closures over their transaction count, the figure the ladder divides the byte cap by.
  • A node is one reporting agent for one pool; one agent name can report for two pools and then counts twice.
  • Eligible EB. An announced EB whose next RB on the chain is 14 or more slots later (15 on the cluster run of 2026-10-17, which ran with that gap). Only eligible EBs can be certified, and the certification rate is certified over eligible.
  • Certified EB. The RB after the announcing one carries the certificate. Closure is the set of transactions the EB references; a node holds the closure when all of them are present.
  • Per node, then the unfavourable-quartile node. The stage rows are computed per node first (a percentile or a share over that node's EBs), then read across nodes at the node on the unfavourable quartile of the statistic, one node one count: the 75th-percentile node for a time and for the applied share, the 25th for the vote rate, so that three nodes in four are faster, apply less, vote more. The two arrival rows add the share of the committee's weight, from the voters file, held by the nodes whose p90 is under the deadline; on the cluster every node weighs the same. Nodes with too few records for a percentile are left out, with the threshold given per entry.
  • Reporting coverage. A pool is observed in a window when its own votes or vote refusals are in the data; the reporting weight is the share of the committee's weight those pools hold, computed per window against that window's total weight (a window that straddles an epoch holds two committees).
  • Percentiles are the 90th and 95th unless stated, interpolated within the sorted values.

The quantities​

Praos fullness and throughput on mainnet (section 1). Fullness is a block's size over the maximum block size of its epoch; a block is full at 95 % of it. Carried bytes per second over a window is the sum of block sizes over the window's seconds, against the Praos expectation of the limit times the active slot coefficient. The congested windows are found by binning blocks into hours and listing the fullest bins. Chain density is RBs over seconds over 0.05.

The ladder (sections 2 and 4). All from the chain over the loaded windows. The observed RB rate is RBs over the windows' seconds. The announced count is RBs with an EB announcement, the eligible count those with the gap, p observed = eligible over announced. The survival of the mix is certified over eligible; the survival of full EBs is the same ratio over EBs whose reference bytes are at least 99 % of the binding capacity, which is S_EB when S_EB binds and S_EB-tx over the transaction size when S_EB-tx does. The transaction size is the certified closures' bytes over their transactions. The load factors are the share of RBs announcing an EB, the mean reference bytes over the binding capacity, and the mean plain RB bytes over the 90,112 B limit. The measured rung is the certified closures' bytes plus the plain RBs' bytes over the seconds. A rung's transactions per second use the same quantities in transactions.

Three rungs read an EB's bytes from its reference count at the window's mean transaction size: the cap that binds, the threshold for a full EB and the EBs' load factor. A window with two transaction sizes is misread by them. In the 318 B window the sizes are 247 and 506 B and S_EB-tx binds for both, so the mean reads the 506 B-heavy EBs, a quarter of the transactions, at 0.6 to 0.8 of their bytes: the full rung then counts the 247 B EBs of 6,200 transactions and up, the ones costliest to validate, and the load-adjusted rung is off by at most that quarter in a direction the chain does not record. The rungs are kept with that reading, and the measured rung, the certified closures' bytes, is exact. In the 1 kB window the sizes are 220 and 1,024 B and bind different caps: a full 1 kB closure reads as 0.8 full and a full small-transaction EB as 3.5 times the cap, so the mean gives no reading, those rungs are left out, and 4.3.1 reads the window by EB class.

EB and closure sizes (section 4). From the chain: the eligible EBs' reference bytes over the reference width the ladder measures, 36 or 37 B, as a transaction count, and the certified closures' bytes; percentiles over the window's EBs.

Body and closure diffusion per node (section 4, two rows). Per node and EB, the first announcement accepted from a peer with its announcement age, the first body acquired with its body age, and the first closure acquired with its closure age, all ages as the node reports them against the slot start. EBs the node forged itself are left out, and so are pairs whose announcement age exceeds 60 s, since a node catching up accepts old announcements and the age would then measure its lag. Per node, over its EBs: the 90th percentile of the body age and of the closure age. Nodes with fewer than 50 EBs are dropped. The row gives the 75th-percentile node's p90 and the share of the committee's weight held by the nodes whose p90 is under the deadline, 3 s for the body, 3 s and 7 s for the closure.

Applied share (section 4). Per node and EB, the EB validated event's counts of transactions applied in full and reapplied; per node, applied over applied plus reapplied over all its EBs, for nodes with 100 or more records; the row is the 75th-percentile node.

Validation after the closure (section 4). Per node and eligible EB, the EB validated event's time minus the later of the closure's arrival and the slot start plus 3 s (the vote window's opening), so the duration counts only the time validation could have been running. Per node the 90th percentile over 100 or more records; the row is the 75th-percentile node.

Vote tally reaches the quorum (section 4). Each vote a node receives carries the node's running tally for the announcing RB. Per node and eligible EB, the age after the slot at which that tally first reached 0.75 of the committee's weight (39 of 51 equal votes on the cluster). Per node the 90th percentile over the EBs where its tally got there, for nodes with 50 or more such EBs; the row is the 75th-percentile node.

Vote rate (section 4). Per node, the EBs it received (as above) on which it cast a vote, over the EBs it received less those it refused because its chain tip did not announce the EB; the row is the 25th-percentile node, the one that three nodes in four vote more than.

Quorums that became certificates (section 4). Over the window's eligible EBs, those whose tally reached 0.75 at some reporting node, and among them those the chain shows certified.

Closure and vote by EB size, in committee weight (sections 4.3 and 4.3.1). Per eligible EB received by 40 or more reporting nodes (announcement age within 60 s), each node's closure age, and each node's pool weight from the window's voters file: the share of the committee's weight held by the nodes that had the closure by 3 s and by 7 s, an EB nobody completed reading zero. The weight that voted by 7 s comes from the voter-stages records of the same window (below), summed over the voters whose vote was in time. Both are over the committee's total weight, so their ceiling is the reporting weight, and the two instruments' node sets for an EB differ slightly, so the vote share can exceed the arrival share by a few thousandths. EBs are binned by their reference count; per bin, the median over EBs of each share and the quartiles of the certified closures' bytes. The saved per-EB rows also keep the ages by which 75, 90 and 95 % of the nodes held the closure. Per EB the nodes that received its body and that completed its closure are counted as well, and the 6,000-and-up bin is split at the reference cap, 8,550 references and up, with the median of those counts (4.3.1). The forgers of those EBs are read by joining the window's EB announcements to their slot leaders, fetched from db-sync, and to the per-EB body counts: pools are grouped by whether they served the body of every eligible EB they announced, of none, or of some, with the EBs at the cap counted in each group.

Validation by EB size class (section 4.3.1). The records of the validation entry, as for 6.1, every EB validated in the loaded hours, binned by the EB's transaction count as the closure-tail sub-table: per bin the records and EBs, the median age of the closure's arrival and the share of records with the closure in hand by the slot plus 3 s, the median validation time after the later of the closure and the slot plus 3 s, the median age at which the record was validated and the share validated within 7 s of the slot. A node that never validated an EB has no record, so the shares are of the validations that completed.

Voter stages (section 8.1). Per EB and per node that scheduled a vote on it (on the cluster, per node that accepted its announcement, the build having no vote-scheduled event), the record of the closure's arrival, the validation and the vote's outcome, taken from the vote-scheduled, closure-acquired, EB-validated and not-voted events over the loaded hours; a node that refused the EB because its own chain tip did not announce it, or found itself off the committee, is not a voter of that EB. Each EB's shares are over its voters, so a voter that never validated the EB counts as lost; a late vote is attributed to one cause, the closure after the deadline or never, the validation past the deadline, or the validation never finished. Binned by the EB's transaction count from the body hits, or from the body's bytes at 36 B per reference where the build has no body-hits event, with the EBs whose late voters exceed a quarter of their voters and, from the chain cache, the certified share of the EBs the chain could certify (the next RB at the certification gap or beyond). With the window's voters file, the committee weight of the voters whose vote was in time is summed per EB and kept as quartiles over the bin's EBs, the cell of Tables 4.7 and 4.9; on the cluster every voter weighs one over the EB's voters.

Never-completed closures by fate (section 4). Per node and EB heard announced within 60 s, whether the closure and the body ever arrived, split by what the chain did with the EB: certified, eligible but not certified, superseded by a next RB inside the gap, or announced by an RB that is not on the chain. Shares are of node-EB pairs over the period.

EB classes and composition of the 1 kB window (section 4.3). Every eligible EB of the window classed by its reference count from the chain; for the certified closures, the transactions of the certifying RB from a db-sync instance with their sizes, counted as the 1 kB kind at 700 B and above and as the small kind under 400 B.

Reporting coverage (sections 3 and 4). As in the conventions: per window, the reporting nodes and the pools they report for, the pools in the committee, and the share of the committee's weight held by the pools whose votes or refusals are in the data.

RB kinds (section 5). From the chain: certifying or plain by the certificate flag, announcing or not by the presence of an announced EB, and a plain RB's fullness by its size, full from 80 kB and small under 20 kB.

Adoption delay by RB kind (section 5). Per node and RB on the final chain, the node's first "added to current chain" event for that RB minus the RB's slot start. A certifying RB is binned by the closure it certifies, read as the closure bytes of its parent's EB; a plain RB by its size. The figures are the median, the 90th percentile and the share within 1 s over all node-RB pairs of the window. On the cluster the switch-to-fork event counts as adoption too.

Consecutive-slot pairs and forks (section 5). On the chain, a pair is two RBs at consecutive block heights whose slots are one apart. A forked pair is an orphan, an RB that two or more observed nodes adopted and that is not on the chain, whose competitor on the chain at the same height sits exactly one slot before or after it. The pair's first RB is the one in the earlier slot; its kind is read from the chain when it won and, when it lost, from the orphan's size in the block-fetch completion event, a certifying RB fetching at about 1 kB and a plain one under load at 88 kB. Orphans adopted by a single node are left out as a node on its own stale chain. On the cluster the orphan's size comes from its forger's adoption event.

Leader slots and chain density (section 5). The observed hosts' leader-slot and forged-RB events counted per window; on the testnet only the 13 IOG and PIR producers log them. Chain density is the RBs on the chain over the window's slots over 0.05. The lottery's mean is the slots times 0.05 and its standard deviation the square root of the slots times 0.05 times 0.95, both in RBs. The orphans of the pairs entry are counted per window.

The forge path (section 5.3). From the call traces of the three IOG producers: for each RB the host forged that is on the chain, the durations of the forge call and of its steps (the mempool snapshot, the partition of the mempool, the block forging, the EB announcement), keyed by the slot the call was made for, and the forged event's time minus the slot start. The closure resolution step is read on all 13 hosts from the call keyed by the EB announcement it resolved, binned by that closure's bytes, with the cost per transaction as the median duration over the median transaction count of the bin. Medians and 90th percentiles are pooled over the hosts' RBs in the loaded windows.

The adoption path (section 5.3). Per node and RB on the chain, the block-fetch completion (body arrival), the chain-selection start (the block popped from the queue) and the adoption, as ages after the slot start, and the validation inside chain selection as the valid-candidate event's time minus the popped time; on the IOG hosts that duration equals the chain-selection call's own duration RB by RB. Medians and 90th percentiles over node-RB pairs; the cluster reads the same events from its chain-database traces.

Validation time by applied share (section 6.1). The records of the validation entry above, every EB a node validated whether it certified or not, with the duration between 0 and 60 s and at least 100 transactions; the applied share is applied over applied plus reapplied. The EBs are classed by their transaction count in steps of 500 and the class holding the most distinct EBs is kept, so that every cell compares EBs of one size. Records are binned by applied share (0 to 10 %, 10 to 30, 30 to 50, 50 to 70, 70 to 100). For each IOG and PIR host the median duration per bin, printed per instance class as the range of its hosts' medians, with the records in brackets; a cell is marked t, tentative, when a host in it has fewer than 30 records and left blank when every host has fewer than 10. For the operators, the nodes with 20 or more records in every bin are kept, the same set in every column; per node the median per bin, then the median and the quartiles across nodes. On the cluster all 51 nodes form the one population.

EBs failing by applied share (section 6.3). On a cluster run, per EB the mean over its voters of the applied share from the same records, binned (under 30 %, then steps of 10 to 70 and over); per bin the EBs and the share that failed to certify, an EB certifying when 39 or more of the 51 voters had it validated by 7 s after the slot.

Nodes by their closure p90 (section 6.4). The per-node closure percentiles of the diffusion entry above (nodes with 50 or more EBs), counted in bins of the node's own p90 (under 2 s, 2 to 3, 3 to 4, 4 to 7, 7 to 15, 15 and over), with the median node's p90 and never-completed share in each population's diffusion_bins.csv.

Upstream-peer groups (section 6.4). A node's upstream peers are the distinct peers that delivered its EB announcements during the window, counted from the peer field of its accepted-announcement events; for the IOG and PIR hosts, whose events carry no peer, the same count is taken from the monitoring's per-peer announcement events over the same period. Memory and CPU (section 6.5). Operators: from each node's metric scrapes (about one per minute) the resident set at the node's median and peak sample, the GC live data at its peak, the process CPU as cores from the differences of the cumulative tick counter over intervals of 60 s or more (shorter intervals quantise), taking the node's median and 90th-percentile interval, with counters that imply more than 32 cores dropped as a different unit, and the GC share as the positive increments of the GC counter over those of the CPU counter; then the distribution across nodes. IOG and PIR hosts: the same from the monitoring's per-minute series, plus the host's total memory and its five-minute CPU busy fraction. Cluster: the same from the per-second resource trace.

Node versions. The build information metric of each node's scrapes, per day, keeping the revision seen last on a day with an upgrade.

The ledger benchmark by processor (section 7.1). The survey's rows grouped by the processor model as reported; a model's cell is the range over its machines, and the summary rows the minimum, quartiles, median and maximum over all machines. The machines' logical CPU counts and virtualisation are in the printer's expanded output.

The cost per transaction at the vote (section 6.2). The records of the validation entry, as for 6.1, on the EBs of one size class and, on the testnet, on the certified closures the db-sync rows select: by input origin, the closures with 80 % or more of their inputs spending an output of the same block (chained) and those with 50 % or less (old-input), or by transaction size, the closures whose transactions all lie in a band. A certifying RB is mapped to its EB through the chain cache. Per host, the cost per reapplied transaction is the median over the records in which the host applied no transaction of the closure of the validation time over the transaction count; the cost per applied transaction the median over the records in which it applied half or more of the time net of the reapplied transactions at that rate, over the applied count. Hosts are printed by instance class as the range of their medians, with the number of EBs behind each reading in brackets; a cell resting on fewer than 10 EBs on some host of the class reads "too few EBs" in the report, its cost kept in the instrument's output and the printer's expanded form; the operators' quartiles are over one node set under both selections, the nodes with 10 or more EBs behind the apply reading and 20 or more behind the reapply reading under each, so that the two columns compare the same nodes. The cluster runs (section 7.2) take the most common 500-transaction class and no selection, every node in one population. The size series, printed by the cost printer's series command and read in section 6.2, repeats the readings per EB size class on the 2 MB window with the chained closures of 200 to 260 B transactions and on the cluster runs with the classes they populate.

The repricings (section 8). Both repricings take the per-record validation output of 6.1 and re-run the vote rule on altered validation times. On the cluster, each record's time after its start (the later of the closure's arrival and the 3 s mark) is split between the apply and the reapply operations in proportion to the record's counts at the run's measured costs (Table 7.2) and each part scaled by a factor: the hardware factors of Table 6.2 on both, or the chained-to-old-input ratio on the apply part alone (the loop fix); a row may start the validation at the closure's arrival instead. A voter votes if its scaled time is within 7 s of the EB's slot; an EB certifies with 39 voters, the run's quorum. Per row the cluster repricing prints the time at which the voter completing the quorum has validated (p50 and p90 over EBs) and the EB's transactions scaled by the share of the window that voter used, the closure arriving as it did; throughput is the ladder's formula of 4.1 with the EB term at the certified bytes per eligible EB and the plain-RB term at the certification share, pinned to the run's measured figure, at the run's transaction size. On the testnet the reporting voters hold 61 to 75 % of the committee weight and the rest cannot be repriced, so the repricing reads the votes and projects no certification: it takes the per-vote records of the voter stages above, one per vote a node scheduled, and gives each a validation time per row, the later of the closure's arrival and the 3 s mark plus a duration. Rows: the recorded duration; every voter slower than the current generation scaled down by the ratio of the class's to the node's cost per reapplied transaction (the hardware factor, read on reapply because it does not depend on the input origin; a node without a reapply reading uses its apply cost, else is not moved); and the pessimistic rebuild, the duration rebuilt as 20 ms plus the applied transactions at a class's old-input apply cost and the reapplied ones at the class's reapply cost, with the loop's cost removed as the same rebuild at the class's chained apply cost. A vote whose closure arrived after the deadline or never is late in every row; one whose validation never finished has no counts and stays so. Per row and per EB size bin it prints the votes in time, the votes out of time with the closure in hand, when those votes' closures arrived (p50 and p90), and the duration's p50, p90 and maximum over the votes.

The changed host against its control (section 9.2). From the archived voting events of the two hosts, per host and EB the same record as the validation pairs: the closure's arrival, the validated time, the validation time after the later of the closure and the 3 s mark, the applied and reapplied counts, and the vote's outcome. Per host the least-squares fit of the validation time to the two counts with a constant gives the apply and reapply cost per transaction, and the EBs applying 500 or more with fewer reapplied than a third of that give the validation time per applied transaction at the median. The validation time by applied count bins each host's EBs by its own count, since the two apply different shares of the same EBs. The votes are counted over the EBs the host's chain tip still announced at the vote, with the missed ones split by whether the closure arrived after the deadline or the validation ran past it.

Where each quantity is computed​

The repository draws one boundary. Its inputs are the data sets of DATA.md, each cited by source, period and checksum: the chain cache and the committee, the period databases of the testnet windows with the IOG monitoring merged in, the cluster runs' trace tables and reconstructed chains, the archived monitoring pulls of the 2 MB period, and the mainnet set. Acquiring and building them, which means reaching the explorer, the telemetry archive, the IOG monitoring and the db-sync instance, or transforming their raw files into those data sets, is done outside the repository and is described in the sources, not shipped as code. Inside the repository are the instruments, which read a data set and write a saved output per window or run, the saved outputs themselves, and the printers, which read the outputs and print the report's tables; every number in a table is reproducible from the data sets by running the instrument and then the printer. In the published copy the per-node outputs of the testnet windows and the hosts table carry every node and pool as a number (n1, n2, … and p1, p2, …, one number per node across the windows) in place of the agent names, hostnames and pool ids, with the operators' tickers emptied and the IOG and PIR labels kept; the printers read them unchanged, and the mapping is held by the author and available to reviewers on request.
